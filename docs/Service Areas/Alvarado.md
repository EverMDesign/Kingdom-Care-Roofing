# Alvarado, TX — Service Area Research Profile

## 1. City Overview

- **County**: Johnson County
- **Location relative to Kingdom Care Roofing & Construction**: Approximately 11 miles south-southeast of Kingdom Care's Burleson, TX base, via I-35W
- **Nearest major roads/highways**: Interstate 35W, U.S. Highway 67, U.S. Highway 81, FM 2738
- **Population**: Approximately 5,500–6,000 (current estimate); grew from 3,288 at the 2000 Census to 4,739 at the 2020 Census — a roughly 44% increase over two decades, reflecting the broader Johnson County growth corridor spreading south from Fort Worth/Burleson along I-35W
- **Incorporation**: Incorporated in 1885 (settled earlier; Alvarado is the oldest city in Johnson County)
- **General character**: A historic railroad town turned bedroom community. Alvarado retains a small-town Main Street core while absorbing new rooftop growth from DFW's southward expansion along the I-35W corridor.

## 2. Neighborhoods and Subdivisions

- **Historic Downtown Alvarado**: Core original townsite, platted in the 1880s railroad era — oldest housing stock in the city, much of it pre-1950s with a mix of original wood-frame homes; roofing here is a mix of older 3-tab asphalt replacements over the decades and some remaining original roof decking that may need full tear-off.
- **Established in-town residential streets (near City Hall/College St corridor)**: Primarily mid-20th-century (1950s–1980s) single-family homes, most now on at least a second or third generation of asphalt composition shingle roofs — a strong zone for standard reroof and upgrade-to-architectural-shingle conversations.
- **Newer rooftop growth along FM 2738 and the I-35W frontage**: Subdivisions built from the 2000s forward house newer construction, more likely to carry dimensional/architectural shingles from the builder and present opportunities for Class 4 impact-resistant upgrades at the next replacement cycle.
- **Rural/acreage properties surrounding the city limits**: Common in Johnson County's unincorporated fringe — mix of manufactured homes, older farmhouses, and newer custom builds on larger lots, with correspondingly mixed roofing age and material.

## 4. History

- Alvarado was founded in 1881 and named for Juan Nepomuceno Alvarado, a Mexican governor of California.
- Two rail lines reached the town in short order — the Missouri Pacific Railroad in 1881 and the Chicago, Texas and Mexican Central Railroad in 1884 — triggering a local economic boom.
- By 1885, the year of incorporation, Alvarado had multiple churches, two schools, two cotton gins, an opera house, a bank, and a newspaper, with a population of roughly 2,000.
- The Masonic Institute in town evolved into the Alvarado Normal Institute (1899) and then Alvarado High School (1908–09), establishing the long-running local school identity.
- Through the later 20th century Alvarado remained a modest agricultural and rail-town community; growth accelerated from the 2000s onward as I-35W development pushed south from Fort Worth and Burleson, bringing new subdivisions and commercial development to the city's edges while the historic downtown core was preserved.

## 5. Weather & Storm Patterns

- **Climate baseline**: Humid subtropical climate typical of North Texas — hot summers, mild winters, and a pronounced spring/early-summer severe weather season.
- **Hail risk**: Johnson County sits within the broader DFW hail corridor. 2016 was the costliest hail/thunderstorm season on record for North Texas, with roughly 500,000 hail claims filed statewide and insured losses exceeding $4 billion; a March 23, 2016 storm alone caused over $1 billion in DFW-area damage. Johnson County is consistently ranked among the top Texas counties for wind and hail insurance claims (2007–2017 data).
- **Tornado risk**: The DFW metro area has recorded roughly 350 tornadoes since 1952. The December 26, 2015 outbreak (12 confirmed tornadoes, including the Garland–Rowlett EF4) caused an estimated $1.55 billion in damage — the costliest tornado event in DFW metro history — and underscores the region's late-December/spring tornado risk.
- **Wind**: Straight-line thunderstorm winds and derecho-type events are common in spring, frequently causing shingle lift and blow-off damage independent of hail.
- **Roofing material impact**: The combination of large hail and high straight-line wind exposure makes impact rating a central material decision in this market — standard 3-tab shingles underperform here relative to architectural (laminate) shingles and Class 4 impact-resistant shingles, which can also qualify homeowners for insurance premium discounts in Texas.

## 6. Roofing & Construction Industry Relevance

- **Materials by era**: Pre-1980s homes in historic Alvarado are likely on multiple generations of 3-tab asphalt shingle replacements, with some original wood-frame structures. 1980s–2000s homes are mostly standard 3-tab or early architectural shingle. Post-2000s rooftop growth along FM 2738/I-35W is predominantly builder-grade architectural (dimensional) shingle.
- **Replacement/upgrade sweet spot**: Homes in the 20–25-year range (common among the mid-century in-town housing stock) are prime candidates for full reroofs; newer architectural-shingle homes approaching their first major hail event are good candidates for Class 4 impact-resistant upgrades at claim time.
- **Insurance claim patterns**: Johnson County is a historically high-frequency wind/hail claim county within the DFW market; claim activity spikes follow named regional hail events (2016 and similar seasonal outbreaks).
- **HOA considerations**: Alvarado's housing stock is mostly non-HOA legacy and acreage/rural properties; newer platted subdivisions along the growth corridor may carry HOA architectural guidelines governing shingle color/type — verify per-subdivision before quoting upgrades.
- **Permit authority**: City of Alvarado City Hall, 104 W. College St., Alvarado, TX 76009 — phone **(817) 790-3351**. Building permits and code enforcement for roofing/construction work are handled through City Hall (rural/unincorporated county properties fall under Johnson County authority).
- **Code requirements**: Texas has no single mandatory statewide residential building code — the state adopted the International Residential Code (IRC) as the default standard, but enforcement is left to each incorporated city. Alvarado, as an incorporated city, administers its own adopted building code and permitting process; always confirm current IRC edition and wind-speed design requirements with City Hall before scoping a reroof.
